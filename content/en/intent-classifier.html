Sending every request to the biggest model is slow; sending every request to the smallest is wrong. A router decides, and the usual first router is a list of keywords. We measured ours against something simpler and against a small trained classifier. The numbers changed the design.

What you get. A router right 84 times out of 100 instead of 64, answering in about 20 ms on the processor, and a method to know whether yours beats the simplest possible rule.

1. Measure against the dumb baseline first

On 237 real requests, each labelled easy, standard or hard, in cross-validation:

RouterCorrect
random, respecting class frequencies59.5 %
keyword matching63.7 %
always "standard"72.6 %
trained classifier84.0 %

The real bar was not random: it was "always answer the most common class". Our keyword router was below it. Measure that baseline before celebrating anything.

2. Where the gain came from

The "easy" requests: keywords found 2 out of 35, the classifier 34 out of 35, with no false positive. That is where small models can take over, and where the speed comes from. Latency: 18 to 27 ms for one request, 3.8 ms each in a batch.

3. Three decisions the numbers imposed

  • No confidence fallback. The classifier beat keywords at every confidence level, so falling back to keywords when unsure made things worse. The fallback only serves when the classifier is down.
  • A calibration was rejected although it scored 86.9 %: it never predicted "hard" again. Three points bought by abandoning the class whose mistakes cost quality.
  • "Hard" stays weak (13 out of 30): thirty examples are not enough. Collect more before trusting it.

4. The bigger lesson: the tool catalogue

Measuring the requests revealed something else: our tool catalogue weighed 19,157 tokens for 58 tools, paid at every turn. Loading tools by intent saves about 73 %. And our coding agents deliver merged pull requests with six tools (read, write, edit, search, list, shell): 2,182 tokens. The question stopped being "how to reduce" and became "why 58".

Traps

llama-server ignores enable_thinking at the root of the request: the model thought for 844 characters and answered nothing. Only chat_template_kwargs: {"enable_thinking": false} turns it off (1.7 s instead of 9.6). Through some gateways the root form works, directly it does not.

A router with no logging never improves: record each request and the model that answered, or your training set never grows.
