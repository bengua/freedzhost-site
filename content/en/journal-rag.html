The most expensive problems in a homelab are the ones you solve twice: you fixed it in September, forgot how, and spend October rediscovering it. A journal fixes that only if you can find things in it. A RAG (retrieval-augmented generation) does: it finds the passages of your notes closest to a question, and hands them to you, or to an agent, before anyone guesses.

A bit of history

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) was named in a 2020 paper from Facebook AI Research: instead of hoping a model remembers a fact, search for it in your own documents and hand the passages to the model with the question. A model knows what it read during training; RAG lets it answer from what you wrote yesterday.

1. Write it down

One Markdown file per day, one section per problem: the symptom, the cause, the fix, and the trap. Write it the moment it is solved; tomorrow you will have forgotten the detail that mattered. Our rule: a diagnosis that is not written down will be paid for twice.

2. Index it automatically

A small service splits the notes into chunks, turns each chunk into a vector with a small embedding model (ours: e5-small, which runs on a processor), and answers questions with the closest chunks. A systemd .path unit watches the journal folder and re-indexes when a file changes, about 70 seconds per pass on our small VM.

3. Ask it first

Give your agents a tool that queries it, and make it a rule: before diagnosing anything about the homelab, ask the journal. Close the query endpoint with a token (a header, not in the URL); keep the health check public, a probe should not need a secret.

Traps we paid for

Chunk size. Our embedding model reads 512 tokens, about 1,400 characters of French. Our first chunks were whole sections, some 7,000 characters long: only their beginning was indexed, and an incident written at character 2,719 could never be found. Cap chunks at about 1,200 characters.

The index lives in memory. Our service loaded each index once and kept it for its whole life: re-indexing updated the disk, not the answers. Restart the service after every index, as part of the same job.

The orphan. An old copy of the service kept the port. The new one failed to start, in a loop, and the orphan went on answering with a 40-hour-old index: restart "succeeded", health was green. The tell: every result had the same score, 1.0, so the ranking was just file order. Check who really holds the port (ss -lntp), and stop the orphan by its PID, never by a name pattern.

Memory. Re-indexing peaked at 1 GB on a 2 GB VM with no swap, and the system killed it, every time, silently. 2 GB of swap fixed it without touching the VM's RAM.
